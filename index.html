Is this safe?

Fair question, and the honest answer is worth more than a reassuring one.

The zip is the safer download — unzip it into a folder of its own and run Meridian.exe inside. A game folder is nine items, because that is what a Windows build is: the exe, the engine, the assets, the runtime and the graphics libraries. Unzipped straight onto a desktop, all nine land on the desktop.

The .exe is the same build wrapped in an installer: it asks where to put the game, always gives it a folder of its own, writes a Desktop shortcut and a Start-menu entry, and registers a working uninstaller in Add/Remove Programs. It asks for no administrator rights — it installs under your own user folder and touches nothing else. Your saved towns live in AppData and survive an uninstall.

Because it is not code-signed, Windows SmartScreen will show a blue warning for it (More info → Run anyway). That warning is not evidence of anything except that a small studio has not bought a £300-a-year certificate yet. We would rather tell you that than have you discover it and assume the worst.

Every release publishes SHA-256 checksums so you can prove the file is the one we built. In PowerShell: Get-FileHash .\Meridian-Pre-Alpha-Demo.zip

The flight model is open source and we ship its source with every build. Meridian flies on JSBSim, which is LGPL 2.1, so every release carries jsbsim-source-506773ad.zip — the exact commit our library was built from — and the game’s licence explicitly permits you to modify it, rebuild it, and replace our JSBSim.dll with your own. It is a loose file, never embedded and never DRM-wrapped. You can check that the aeroplane is doing what we say it is doing.

The game makes exactly one network request, at startup, to read a small public file and see whether a newer demo exists. Nothing else is sent, ever, and nothing at all is sent unless you press the report key yourself.

Still not happy? Run it in a sandbox instead — it takes five minutes

This is the right instinct for any unsigned download, not just ours, and we would rather you played it safely than not at all.

Windows Sandbox — the easiest, and free if you have Windows Pro or Enterprise. A throwaway Windows that forgets everything when you close it.

  1. Search Turn Windows features on or off, tick Windows Sandbox, restart.
  2. Open Windows Sandbox from the Start menu.
  3. Copy the Meridian zip into the sandbox window and unzip it there.
  4. Run Meridian.exe. Close the sandbox when you are done and every trace of it is gone.

Graphics inside the sandbox are limited, so the game will look rougher and run slower than it does natively. It is for satisfying yourself that it is harmless, not for judging how it plays.

A full virtual machine — better graphics, a bit more work. Free either way:

  1. Install VirtualBox (free) or VMware Workstation Player.
  2. Get a free Windows 11 evaluation VM from Microsoft — it expires after 90 days, which is plenty.
  3. Give it 8 GB of memory and enable 3D acceleration in the VM's display settings, or the game will crawl.
  4. Take a snapshot before you run anything. That is the whole point: if you dislike what you see, roll back and it never happened.
  5. Drag the zip in, unzip, run.

Or check it without running it at all. Upload the file to VirusTotal — it will be scanned by about seventy engines in a minute. A couple of them flag most unsigned installers on principle; what you are looking for is whether anything reputable names an actual piece of malware.

What you can do in this build

Walk the town on foot. Get into anything — a car, the bus, an aeroplane nobody is flying (F to climb in and out, C to walk to a door). Fly a scout yourself. Follow the fighting from a chase seat. Watch a nation feed itself, put a fitter through his guild and out the other side an engineer, build a bus in a factory and put an air force in the sky, and read why it did any of it.

Set up a custom battle and fly it, or duel another person over the wire (command line for now — see below). What you cannot do yet: play the economy as a settler with your own workers, or take the whole town online. Both are next.

It is pre-alpha and means it. Things are missing, wrong or plainly broken. That is what the demo is for, and a report from you is worth more than a polite silence.

Everything at once

The challenge is not one system done deeply. It is all of them running at the same time, feeding each other, with no pause button. Ore has to come out of a hole before there is steel; steel has to reach a workshop before there are parts; parts have to reach the motor works before there is a lorry to carry the next load of ore. Meanwhile the field has to be reaped, the hands have to be fed and housed, the laboratory wants funding, and there is a war on that consumes the output of all of it. Neglect the roads and the whole chain starves in transit. Neglect the air and you lose the machines the chain exists to build.

Specifics below, because the word "simulation" is cheap. Every one of these is a mechanism you can inspect in game, not a statement of intent.

SystemWhat actually happens
Supply chain Grain, timber, ore, brick, steel, parts. Each is produced somewhere real by somebody named, stored somewhere with a capacity, and consumed by the next stage. A furnace with no ore says so and waits. Nothing is conjured to keep a queue moving.
Transport Goods physically move. Carriers walk them, carts and lorries drive them on roads that had to be planned, staked and laid — with two-way traffic, junction priority and nobody cutting across the verge. A road that stops short is a supply line that stops short.
Flight JSBSim — the open-source six-degree-of-freedom flight dynamics model, the same one FlightGear flies on. Each aeroplane is an XML definition with real mass, inertia, aerodynamic coefficients and a piston engine. The AI and the player fly through identical control inputs; there is no separate arcade path for either of them.
Structure Load factor is measured from the airframe's own motion, not read off a bank angle. A pull-out at 2 g/s makes her complain at about 3.6 g and a spar lets go at 5.6. The warning is a projection — it fires when you are one second from failure at your current rate of onset — leaving about 0.44 s once the filters and the time to centre the stick are paid. A pilot with a 0.20 s reaction lives; one with 0.45 s does not. Overspeed is a separate death: past 145 kt the elevator beats itself off its hinges.
Gunnery No hit rolls anywhere. Rounds are objects with muzzle velocity, time of flight and drop, and they have to physically cross the aeroplane. Dispersion is two terms with different range laws — mount slop, growing linearly, and scatter down the trajectory, growing as range1.5. Dead astern at 100 m a good pilot hits 85% of his rounds; at 400 m, 12%.
Instruments Seven period dials, each reading a named JSBSim property. Press I and every gauge names its exact property path and raw value. A gauge whose source has never moved letters its own face NO READING rather than showing a plausible zero — a missing property reads as zero, and an instrument that lies quietly is worse than one that admits it is blind.
The people About 111 of them by the eighth day, each with a name, a trade, a home, and a day that includes work, the store, the bank and now the pub. They are the labour supply, and they are also the constraint: no beds means no hire, and a hungry town does not build aeroplanes.
The record Every decision writes a row saying what was done and why — who hired whom and what it cost, why a road stopped short, why a pilot broke off. You can read the reasoning behind anything that happened. There are no hidden rolls, because there are no rolls.

Honest note: structural break-off, the new gunnery model, the cockpit instruments and the pub are committed but land in the next build, not the one linked above. The download always fetches the newest release, so they arrive on their own.

Land, work and scrip

The town is not the only economy. Free settlers hold their own land — they build a house of their own, put in plots, farm them, and eat their own grain first. What is over the food reserve they sell to the nation in lots for scrip, and when the scrip is enough they buy the next square of land and do it again. The nation buys, but only above its own meal reserve: it will refuse you if its bins are low, and say so.

Their wealth is counted honestly — grain and timber at the nation's posted rates, plus scrip, plus land at its deed price — and posted once a day under their own name. Every purchase, every sale, every plot is a line you can read.

In this demo you watch that happen and you can walk through it. Being one of them — hiring your own workers, running your own books against theirs — is the next thing, and it is what the whole economy was built to support.

Where this comes from

The Guild, in first person, in the First World War. That is the whole of it in ten words. What was always good about The Guild was that the world did not wait for you: people had trades and families and debts, the town had an economy that ran whether you were looking at it or not, and you were one person inside it rather than a cursor above it. That is the part being taken seriously here. Everyone in Meridian has a name, a trade, a skill sheet they are filling in by doing the work, somewhere to sleep and a reason to be where they are.

The other half is the air war as it actually was — the thing the Red Baron documentaries get right and most games get wrong. Not aeroplanes with hit points, but wood and wire and fabric that comes apart if you pull too hard; guns that miss constantly; and men who mostly died without ever seeing what killed them.

And you make the difference, in the air and on the ground. You can take a machine and fly it. You can walk the town, ride the bus, stand in the square while the president speaks. Neither half is a menu you visit — the aerodrome exists because the town built it, and the town keeps going because the aerodrome holds.

The planet is round

Not a skybox with a sun painted on it. There is a sphere with a radius and a latitude, and everything follows from it:

  • The sun is solved, not animated. Hour angle at 15° per hour — which is the planet turning — declination from the day number by Cooper's equation, and elevation from those two and your latitude. So the seasons are real and latitude genuinely matters: at Meridian's home latitude the sun climbs steeply and the days are near enough twelve hours all year.
  • The stars wheel about the celestial pole, and the pole sits at an altitude equal to your latitude — which is a fact of a round planet rather than a decoration. Some two thousand of them, turning with the same rotation as the sun, seen from the other side.
  • The ground curves away. The far horizon drops by d²/2R against a 6,371 km radius — a hundred and twenty-six metres at forty kilometres — so the world falls away from you as it should, and haze takes what the curvature leaves.
  • Night is not black. Moonlight over a blue-grey ambient, because a night nobody can see in is a night nobody plays through.

Custom battles — you are flying inside two clicks

The first row on the front menu, and the fastest way to find out whether you like the flying before you decide whether you like the town. You set the fight and then you are in it:

Your side, and theirsAny two of the eight founding nations. The colour you fly in is the nation's own livery, so the word on the menu and the paint on the machine are the same fact.
Your machineWhatever the aerodrome actually carries. Not a separate list that can drift from the game — the same aeroplanes the town builds.
How many of themOne to six enemies.
How many of youNone to five friends. None is a fair choice and it is a hard one.
How good they all areNovice, competent, veteran or ace — one dial, and it governs your side and theirs alike. A fight where your wingman is an ace and theirs is a boy is not the fight you chose.

Skill is not a difficulty slider dressed up. It becomes a pilot in exactly one place in the code, so the sheet the menu shows you cannot disagree with the men who take off. An ace sees you further out, reacts in a fifth of a second where a novice needs nearly half, and holds a firing solution you would lose. He does not roll better than you — nothing in Meridian rolls. He is given more room and less time wasted, and what he does with it is flying.

When one side is left standing you get the card: who shot whom, who never fired a round, and how she went down — and a collision says so rather than crediting a man who never pulled a trigger. From there, fly it again or go back and change the fight.

Why this is on the front menu at all, honestly: a long unattended run proved you cannot reach an aeroplane in eighteen game days of ordinary play. The chain from the pithead through the furnace and the workshop to the aerodrome is longer than a fortnight. That is a real fault and it is on the list. Until it is fixed, the air should not be locked behind it.

Multiplayer — fighter duels

Two people, two machines, one sky. That is the whole of it for now, and narrowing it that far was deliberate: one aeroplane each, made to work properly, before a whole town made to work at all.

The other man's aeroplane is told, not guessed at. His stick and rudder come down the wire and are written straight into a flight model on your machine as the very same values that move your own control surfaces — so his ailerons, his elevator and his propeller move on your screen through exactly the code that moves yours. Nothing is animated to look right.

What it costsAbout 1.2 kB/s each way. A dial-up modem would carry it.
What is sentPosition and attitude twenty times a second on the fast, lossy channel; damage and parts coming off on the reliable one, because losing a wing is not something to drop.
Third-party codeNone. Hand-written UDP with sequence numbers and acknowledgements, behind a seam a Steam transport can drop into later. Taking a library would have meant asserting its licence from memory, and this project has been bitten by exactly that once already.

There is no button for it yet. The menu still shows multiplayer greyed out — the wire landed after the menu did. Today it is two copies of the game and two command lines:

Meridian.exe -meridianDuelHost 7777 -meridianName Tyrael
Meridian.exe -meridianDuelJoin 192.168.1.20:7777 -meridianName Penrose

Direct IP. No matchmaking, no lobby, no NAT traversal — the same house, or you forward the port. A button is owed and is next. Not the town over the wire, not co-op: fighter duels.

You fly with them, and against them

The pilots are not targets that move. They see, and how far depends on their eyes — a veteran picks a machine out at a kilometre where a boy does not find it until 340 m. They refuse a head-on merge and work round to your blind side with height in hand. They fly in pairs, and the wingman's whole job is his leader's tail. They check behind themselves — badly, on purpose, because dead astern is a blind spot in an aeroplane with a fuselage and an engine's smoke in the way, which is why four in five men shot down in that war never saw it.

Get behind one and he will do something about it: loop over the top so you go underneath, Split-S away if he has the height to spend, haul into an Immelmann if he has the speed — and which of those he picks depends on the energy he has at that moment, not on a difficulty setting.

And none of it is cheating. Skill changes what goes into the aeroplane — how fast his hands are, how wide his gun scatters, how far he can see, how hard he dares pull before a spar lets go. No pilot in this game rolls a die to decide whether he hits you. When a novice misses, it is because his nose was physically late and his aim was short.

One against one, a full-sheet veteran beats a novice six times out of six — and comes home holed in half of them, because the boy is shooting back. Two veterans against four novices: six wins in ten, with about one of the two coming home.

The nation, and the man who runs it

The head of state is a real person, not a menu. He eats the nation's grain, sleeps in a house, and has health like anybody else. He keeps hours: the decree at seven, written off the state of the nation as it actually is that morning; the capital's door open to anyone who turns up, until noon; a speech in the square at noon that the notables drop what they are doing and travel in for — officers, riggers, the constable, the banker — while the farmhands keep working, because somebody has to. Then state visits in the afternoon to whatever has actually been built: the guild hall, the laboratory, the bank, the hospital. He is driven. The chauffeur has a name, a house and an opinion.

And he can be killed. If the capital falls he dies, and the deputy — who has been kept at a standing cost in grain all along, because a spare head of state is not free — takes the office. With no deputy, the nation joins its conqueror. That is the war's actual objective: not territory, but the other nations' kings.

The trades keep guilds. Ore hewn, miles walked, rows reaped, machines rigged, hours flown — a man's advancement is the work he has already done, and the guild is only where it is recognised. Bring enough of it and the trainer signs his ticket: a fitter walks in and an engineer walks out, wanting different things, able to build things he could not, and worth more to the nation than he was that morning.

How far he can rise is set by how far the nation has pushed the sciences. The laboratory is not scenery and it is not a bonus building — it is the ceiling on what anybody in the country can become. Fund it and your people get better. Ignore it and your best miner stays a very good miner for ever, however many years he gives you.

Not in this build yet: the ranks themselves — apprentice, journeyman, master — and one guild for each trade rather than one hall for all of them.

The legends say that once in a generation, a man surpasses his peers by a thousand years.
Nobody who watched him could describe afterwards what he had done. Only that he did the impossible.
There is a rank above master. One man in the world holds it, he did not study his way there, and that is all you are getting.

How it thinks

  • Nothing is hidden. Every person, machine and building can tell you what it is doing and why it decided that. No secret rolls.
  • Nothing appears from nowhere. A bus is built on a factory floor and drives out of it. Aeroplanes come out of hangars.
  • Nothing overlaps anything. Two things touching is a failure — and now a fatal one.
  • Skill shapes inputs, never outcomes. A better pilot has quicker hands and better eyes, not better luck.

If something goes wrong

Press F8 where it happened. It photographs what is on screen before drawing itself, asks what went wrong, and packages the picture, your note, the log and a census of the whole world into one file. That census is the useful part: where everything is, what everyone is doing, how fast, and how long since anything last moved — which is how a stuck thing gets found.

Nothing is sent anywhere. It writes one zip and opens the folder, and you decide what to do with it.

0.7.2 — 8 September 2026 — she stays where you put her, and there is a tutorial
  • The aeroplanes were tail-heavy and now they are not. The centre of gravity sat behind the point the lift acts through, which is a nose-up couple that never goes away. Hands off with no trim at all, the nose used to wander 46° in half a minute; it wanders 19° now, and the static margin went from 3.3% to 10%.
  • A trim tab, and an automatic one. Both aeroplanes have had the tab in their flight model since the day they were written and nothing had ever moved it. NumPad 7/1 is the wheel; the automatic follows the stick while you hold it and holds the attitude you left her at when you let go. T turns it off.
  • A rudder trim on NumPad 0/. that flies the ball to the middle — worst sideslip over half a minute went from 1.8° by hand to 0.2°.
  • An artificial horizon, which is an anachronism on a 1916 panel and went in anyway: you have no peripheral vision and no seat, and without it you cannot tell a climb from a stall.
  • The altimeter was never wrong — “42850 ft” was 2,850 with the dial’s own numerals printing through the figure. It also shows height above the ground now, which is the number that decides whether you live.
  • The flying lesson was teaching the wrong key. Three steps told you to pull back with NUMPAD 8, and 8 is nose down. Following it exactly would have put the nose into the ground.
  • The tutorial has a first act: the capital is burning, the king is wounded in the back of your car, and the aerodrome is across the valley. Drive him there, through the gate the sentry opens because the car is the nation’s, and park beside the machine. Every step signs itself off from the world — the distance you covered, the gate’s own open flag, whether you actually stopped. No timers, no “press X to continue”.
  • Doors and footsteps make a sound, generated rather than sampled, and deliberately quiet.
  • The front door tells you which build you are running — version, build time and commit — with the same stamp down the edge of the screen, and a button to check for a newer one.
